<p class="intro"><span class="dropcap">W</span>ith regards to emotion, Storycenter founder Joe Lambert emphasizes that audiences respond most strongly when a story carries genuine meaning for the author. Despite my many criticism of Lambert’s bureaucratic model of digital storytelling and his relentless attempts at theorizing the movement, I must admit that I share this particular sentiment, especially when the humanization of marginalized groups is the primary objective.</p>

For this piece, I have chosen to analyze three different girls’ education activists who share stories through varying technological means (some of which are more effective than others), but are united by the fact that their passion for facilitating social change is ignited by their own lived experiences.

#### Varaidzo Kativhu's Honourary Doctorate Speech

<figure>
	<img src="/assets/img/vee-kativhu-img.jpg" alt=""> 
	<figcaption>Vee Kativhu, by * via Wikimedia Commons</figcaption>
</figure>

[Varaidzo Kativhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vee_Kativhu), is a British-Zimbabwean girls education activist who began her advocacy work through posting free educational resources on her personal Youtube channel while pursuing her undergraduate degree at the University of Oxford. As her platform grew, she founded a non-profit organization, Empowered by Vee in hopes to widen access for higher education for students in the U.K which swiftly expanded to serve students across numerous African nations. 

In 2024, Kativhu was awarded an honorary doctorate by the University of Bradford in recognition of her advocacy work and she took the stage to deliver a vulnerable narrative of how her upbringing had led her to that specific moment. Kativhu’s speech speaks to the effectiveness of authenticity in storytelling, as she begins with an account about the adversity she experienced as a young black girl growing up in a low-income, single parent household while feeling unsupported in her secondary school studies.

#### Malala Yousafzai’s Digital Timeline

<figure>
  <img src="/assets/img/malala-img.jpg" alt="Malala Yousafzai in 2015">

Arguably the most influential girls education activist, is none other than Pakistan-born [Malala Yousafzai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malala_Yousafzai), who never stopped using her voice to demand educational policy reform from extremist regimes despite nearly losing her life after a targeted shooting by a masked Taliban gunman. 

The digital story I am featuring is a scroll-driven chronological timeline, presenting significant events and milestones from Yousafzai’s youth to the present, published on the website of her non-profit organization, the [Malala Fund](https://malala.org/malalas-story). 

The timeline, which featured short summaries of events and a handful of corresponding images, rendered the catastrophe that undergirds Yousafzai’s story relatively flat, and the text provided failed to move me the way her personal narration had. I recognize that the purpose of timelines is to help readers synthesize deeply complex chronological events into an easily digestible format, [and can encourage deeper reflexivity among participants](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2772766125000369) and was not designed to be an emotional experience, but one of knowledge transmission. That said, the lack of interactive elements, such the ability to zoom in and out, maps that showed her location from event to event, and hyperlinks that navigated readers to supplementary articles (only one link was featured) left much to be desired.

Returning once again to Parvin’s paper, she argues that modern technologies risk reducing storytelling to a mechanical process of information exchange. I chose to follow up Kativhu’s profoundly touching honorary doctorate speech with the timeline of Yousafzai’s advocacy work in order to emphasize the affordances of auditory digital storytelling formats relative to those that are textual, particularly in instances where social girls education advocacy initiatives are driven by the tribulations and triumphs of a central figure. I, of course, understand that preferences may vary in regards to how media is consumed, and many may find writing to be more authentic than spoken word, but I am firm in my argument that Malala’s inspiring transition from the Taliban’s attack to global advocacy can not be understood in its full complexity through a mere synopsis. 

For those who prefer to read rather than to listen, but still want to experience a gut-wrenching first-person narration similar to Kativhu's speech, Malala has co-written published a autobiographical narrative of her life called "I am Malala," which is a genuinely emotive piece that allows readers to take in the full picture of her story rather than in flattening chunks.
