<p class="intro"><span class="dropcap">W</span>ith respect to emotion, StoryCenter founder Joe Lambert emphasizes that audiences respond most strongly when a story carries genuine meaning for the author. Despite my very open criticism of Lambert’s bureaucratic model of digital storytelling and his relentless attempts at theorizing the movement, I must admit that I share this particular sentiment, especially when the humanization of marginalized groups is the primary objective.</p>

For this piece, I am choosing to analyze three different girls’ education activists who share their vulnerable stories through varying technological means (some of which are definitely more effective than others), but are united by the fact that their passion for facilitating social change is ignited by their own lived experiences. To ground my analysis, I will be leaning heavily on [Nassim Parvin's ideas of how to do justice to stories](https://doi.org/10.17351/ests2018.248), and will critically examine whether the technological tools used to facilitate these stories adequately capture their emotional nuance or render them flat. 

#### [Varaidzo Kativhu's Honourary Doctorate Speech](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IWrlWGZK6MQ&t=440s)

[Varaidzo Kativhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vee_Kativhu), is a British-Zimbabwean girls education activist who began her advocacy journey through posting free educational resources and writing tips on her personal Youtube channel while pursuing her undergraduate degree at the University of Oxford. As her platform grew, she founded a non-profit organization, [Empowered by Vee](https://www.empoweredbyvee.com/) in hopes to widen access for higher education for students in the U.K which quickly expanded to serve students across numerous African nations. 

In 2024, Kativhu was awarded an honorary doctorate degree by the [University of Bradford](https://www.bradford.ac.uk/) in recognition of her advocacy work and she took the stage to deliver a vulnerable narrative of how her upbringing had led her to that specific moment. Kativhu’s speech speaks to the effectiveness of authenticity in storytelling, as she begins with an account about the adversity she experienced as a young black girl growing up in a low-income, single parent household while feeling unsupported in her secondary school studies. 

I recognize that an address given to a live audience may not be what initially comes to mind when deciding what counts as a digital story, since commencement speeches are not typically published online for the general public and the dialogue can often feel anchored to that moment in time. With that said, some definitions of digital storytelling suggest thst the movement should be defined by what it does rather than it is (which makes perfect sense when discussing emotional response), so I’d like to emphasize that Kativhu makes a conscious choice to digitally archive her academic and philanthropic achievements through her social media platforms and does so with the understanding that her story can instill a love of learning in audience members who are a part of underrepresented groups and insist that they to take up space in academia.

In Nassim Parvin’s paper _Doing Justice to Stories_, she argues that digital stories hold inherent value as an archive, and serve as resources that can be mined for a variety of purposes. Parvin is absolutely correct in her sentiments as thousands of racialized students benefit from Kativhu’s archive of educational resources and view her as a support network that they may not possess in their personal lives.

I can personally attest to the emotional effectiveness of Kativhu’s diaristic storytelling, as I was one of the many students who "mined" her archive of free educational resources when I could not afford tutoring in secondary school, and it (although parasocial) was through her encouragement, that I felt empowered to pursue university studies despite my complex financial situation.

In addition to Kativhu’s voice, an affordance of video recordings is that audiences can view corresponding facial expressions of the storyteller and the listeners. A key example occurs when Kativhu, who is visibly emotional, mentions the role that her family has played in her success and how she dedicates her newly received degree to her mother. The camera immediately pans to Varaidzo’s mother **(see 8:06)** whose tear-stricken expression reflects the emotional toll of single parenthood coupled with immense pride in her child who is actively flipping the script for marginalized groups, and serves as a reminder that behind all growth narratives are real people who have experienced hardship. If that is not an example of preserving the human element in digital storytelling, I do not know what is. 

I do not believe for a second that it is possible to convey these emotions in a medium that does not feature Kativhu’s spoken word, and I struggle to imagine an instance where I would finish this video without feeling irrevocably inspired.

Arguably the most influential girls education activist, is none other than Pakistan-born [Malala Yousafzai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malala_Yousafzai), who never stopped using her voice to demand educational policy reform from extremist regimes despite nearly losing her life after a targeted shooting by a masked Taliban gunman. 

I am featuring a scroll-driven chronological timeline, presenting significant events and milestones from Yousafzai’s youth to the present, published on the website of her non-profit organization, the [Malala Fund](https://malala.org). 

The timeline, which featured relatively short summaries of events and a handful of corresponding images, rendered the catastrophe that undergirds Yousafzai’s story relatively flat, and reading the text provided failed to move me the way her personal narration likely would. Don't get me wrong, I recognize that the purpose of timelines is to help readers synthesize deeply complex chronological events into an easily digestible format, [and can encourage deeper reflexivity among participants](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2772766125000369) and was not designed to be an emotional experience, but one of knowledge transmission. That said, the lack of interactive elements that typically make timelines engaging, such the ability to zoom in and out, maps that plot where exactly the events took place, and hyperlinks that navigate readers to supplementary articles (only one link was featured) left much to be desired.

Returning once again to Parvin’s paper, she argues that modern technologies risk reducing storytelling to a mechanical process of information exchange, a sentiment that is unfortunately applicable to this example. I chose to follow up Kativhu’s profoundly touching honorary doctorate speech with the timeline of Yousafzai’s advocacy work in order to emphasize the greater affordances of auditory digital storytelling formats relative to those that are textual, particularly in instances where social girls education advocacy initiatives are driven by the tribulations and triumphs of a central figure. I, of course, understand that preferences may vary in regards to how media is consumed, and many may find writing to be more authentic than spoken word, but I stand firm in my argument that Malala’s inspiring transition from the Taliban’s attack to global advocacy absolutely cannot be understood in its full complexity through a mere textual synopsis. 

For those who prefer to read rather than to listen, but still want to experience a heart-wrenching first-person narration similar to Kativhu's speech, Malala has co-written published a autobiographical narrative of her life called ["I am Malala,"](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/17851885-i-am-malala) which is a genuinely emotive read that in my opinion, allows readers to take in the full picture of her story rather than in flattening chunks!

To conclude my analysis, I will be featuring Nigerian-American, [Zuriel Oduwole](https://www.zurieloduwole.com/), who began creating short films at the age of ten in hopes of influencing discussions on education across Africa. This particular short film called _Being Unstoppable_ is about how emotional confidence and menstrual education are integral to keeping African girls in school. 

I felt that it would be best to save Oduwole’s short film for last, I wanted to touch on how her drive to engage in advocacy differs slightly from Kativhu and Yousafzai’s in the sense that she is not speaking from the personal. As Lambert would put it, Odowole’s short film is an example of [a story that originates from fleeting moments of encounter](https://www.storycenter.org/inventory/p/digital-storytelling-story-work-for-urgent-times) rather than autobiographical adversity, and I feel that these kinds of stories, when digitized, can potentially dilute the emotional response that is expected of audiences. To provide some additional context, Odowole was born and raised in a middle-class neighbourhood in Los Angeles and became interested in advocacy work after flying to West Africa to complete a school project, where she was surprised by the shocking number of young girls wandering the streets during school hours. It was that specific moment in time that decided she was going to make a difference.

I want to quickly note that I absolutely do not intend to minimize the impact of Odowole’s advocacy work! A young woman of colour who is committed to educational policy reform in African nations through filmmaking, is nothing short of inspiring.  I am simply trying to point out this may be grounds for a possible emotional disconnect upon the discovery that the work is informed through observation instead of lived adversity.

Another element that makes this digital story unique from the previous two is that it was facilitated through a brand partnership campaign with [_Always_](https://www.always.com/en-us), a global brand of menstrual hygiene products. A co-sign from a multinational brand provides Odowole with a political and social realm more powerful than her own, which is obviously great in terms of generating impressions from those who would have not come across her advocacy work organically.

 Although Oduwole’s film contains all the elements of an immersive story (her own voiceover, an uplifting soundtrack, footage of African girls in school) and showcases her genuine passion for enacting meaningful social change, I cannot help but feel as if the emotional impact was flattened through the knowledge that the story is an advertisement **(see 0:51)**, where a classroom full of students is shown with packages of _Always_-branded pads on their laps.

Parvin warns that modern technologies used in digital storytelling can turn them into commodities, and the brand clearly understood that the campaign would garner more support and emotional investment through the platforming of a youth education advocate who has received worldwide recognition. Odowule is undoubtedly a talented filmmaker, and I have no doubts that the media she produces entirely on her own where she is in full control of narration and imagery, would do far more justice to the stories of African girls out of school, than a commercial ever could.
